Section 8.18 • Fundamental Concept

What is Acceleration Due to Gravity ($g$)?

The force of attraction exerted by the Earth on a body is called gravity. Whenever a body falls freely towards the surface of the Earth under the action of gravity alone, its velocity increases at a constant rate.

Definition: The uniform acceleration produced in a freely falling body due to the gravitational pull of the Earth is called acceleration due to gravity and is denoted by the letter $g$.
SI Unit: $\text{m s}^{-2}$ or $\text{N kg}^{-1}$ Dimensions: $[\text{M}^0\text{L}^1\text{T}^{-2}]$ Standard Earth Value: $g \approx 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$ Direction: Towards Centre $O$ of Earth
Crucial Concept: Vacuum vs. Air

When two bodies of different masses or volumes fall from the same height in vacuum, they reach the Earth at the exact same time because $g$ is independent of the falling body's mass.

In an air medium, if two bodies of the same mass but different volumes fall from the same height, the body of smaller volume reaches earlier because the upward thrust (buoyancy/drag) due to air on the smaller volume is less than that on the larger volume.

Textbook Sample Problem

The radius of the moon is $1.7 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ and its mass is $7.4 \times 10^{22}\text{ kg}$. If $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}$, find the value of acceleration due to gravity on the surface of moon.

Given: $R_m = 1.7 \times 10^6\text{ m}$, $M_m = 7.4 \times 10^{22}\text{ kg}$, $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}$
Formula: $$g_m = \frac{G M_m}{R_m^2}$$
Substitution: $$g_m = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 7.4 \times 10^{22}}{(1.7 \times 10^6)^2} = \frac{49.358 \times 10^{11}}{2.89 \times 10^{12}} \approx 1.71\text{ m s}^{-2}$$
Note: $g_{\text{moon}} \approx \frac{1}{6} g_{\text{earth}}$.
Section 8.18 • Step-by-Step Derivation

Relation Between $g$ and $G$

Step 1: Consider the Earth-Body System

Consider the Earth to be a spherical body of mass $M$ and radius $R$ with centre $O$. Suppose a body of mass $m$ is placed on the surface of the Earth (Fig. 8.9), where acceleration due to gravity is $g$. The entire mass $M$ of the Earth can be considered concentrated at its centre $O$.

Step 2: Apply Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation

Let $F$ be the gravitational force of attraction between the body of mass $m$ and the Earth of mass $M$ separated by distance $R$:

$$F = \frac{G M m}{R^2}$$
Step 3: Apply Newton's Second Law (Gravity Pull / Weight)

By definition, the gravitational pull on the body produces acceleration due to gravity $g$, which equals the weight of the body:

$$F = m g$$
Step 4: Equate Both Expressions for Force $F$

Equating the two expressions for $F$, we get:

$$m g = \frac{G M m}{R^2}$$
Fundamental Relation (Equation 11)
$$g = \frac{G M}{R^2}$$
Step 5: Relation in Terms of Uniform Density ($\rho$) of Earth

If $\rho$ is the uniform (average) density of the material of the Earth, then Mass $=$ Volume $\times$ Density: $$M = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho$$ Substituting $M$ in $g = \frac{GM}{R^2}$:

$$g = \frac{G \times \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho}{R^2} \implies \mathbf{g = \frac{4}{3}\pi G R \rho}$$

Using $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$, $R = 6.4 \times 10^6\text{ m}$, and $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}$, average density of Earth is $\rho \approx 5.5 \times 10^3\text{ kg m}^{-3}$.

💡
Important Note (From Eq. 11)

From $g = \frac{GM}{R^2}$, we note that the value of acceleration due to gravity $g$ is completely independent of the mass ($m$), shape, and size of the falling body, but depends upon the mass ($M$) and radius ($R$) of the planet/heavenly body. Hence, the value of $g$ on different heavenly bodies is different.

Section 8.20 (a) • Effect of Altitude

Variation of Acceleration Due to Gravity ($g$) with Altitude (Height $h$)

As we move upwards above the surface of the Earth to a height $h$, our distance from the centre $O$ of the Earth increases from $R$ to $(R + h)$. Consequently, the force of gravity and the value of acceleration due to gravity decrease with altitude. It is due to this reason that the value of $g$ is lesser at mountains than in plains!

Section 8.20 (a) • Complete Textbook Derivation

Step-by-Step Derivation: Effect of Altitude ($h$)

Step 1: Acceleration Due to Gravity on Surface (Point $A$)

Consider the Earth to be a sphere of mass $M$, radius $R$ with centre at $O$. Let $g$ be the value of acceleration due to gravity at a point $A$ on the surface of the Earth (Fig. 8.10a):

$$g = \frac{G M}{R^2} \qquad \text{...(15)}$$
Step 2: Acceleration Due to Gravity at Height $h$ (Point $B$)

If $g'$ is the acceleration due to gravity at a point $B$ at a height $h$ above the surface of the Earth, then the distance of point $B$ from the centre $O$ is $(R + h)$. Since the Earth's mass $M$ is concentrated at $O$:

$$g' = \frac{G M}{(R + h)^2} \qquad \text{...(16)}$$
Step 3: Divide Equation (16) by Equation (15)

Dividing (16) by (15), we obtain the general relation valid for all heights $h$:

$$\frac{g'}{g} = \frac{G M}{(R + h)^2} \times \frac{R^2}{G M} = \frac{R^2}{(R + h)^2} \qquad \text{...(17)}$$

Taking $R^2$ common from the denominator:

$$\frac{g'}{g} = \frac{R^2}{R^2\left(1 + \frac{h}{R}\right)^2} = \left(1 + \frac{h}{R}\right)^{-2}$$
Step 4: Binomial Approximation when $h \ll R$

If $h \ll R$, then $\frac{h}{R}$ is very small as compared to $1$. Expanding the R.H.S. of the above equation by the Binomial Theorem $(1 + x)^n = 1 + nx + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!}x^2 + \dots$ and neglecting the square and higher powers of $\frac{h}{R}$, we get:

$$\frac{g'}{g} \approx 1 - \frac{2h}{R}$$
Altitude Formula for $h \ll R$ (Equation 18)
$$g' = g\left(1 - \frac{2h}{R}\right) \qquad \text{...(18)}$$
Important Notes (From Reference Book)
  • (i) When to use Eq. (17) vs Eq. (18):
    Relation (17) $g' = \frac{g R^2}{(R+h)^2}$ is used when $h$ is comparable to $R$ (e.g., $h > 500\text{ km}$). Relation (18) $g' = g\left(1 - \frac{2h}{R}\right)$ is used only when $h \ll R$ (e.g., $h = 10\text{ km}$ or $50\text{ km}$).
  • (ii) At $h = R = 6400\text{ km}$:
    From (17), $g' = \frac{g R^2}{(R + R)^2} = \frac{g}{4}$.
  • (iii) Decrease, Fractional & Percentage Decrease ($h \ll R$):
    • Decrease in value of $g$: $$\Delta g = g - g' = \frac{2 h g}{R}$$
    • Fractional decrease in $g$: $$\frac{g - g'}{g} = \frac{2h}{R}$$
    • % decrease in value of $g$: $$\left(\frac{g - g'}{g}\right) \times 100 = \frac{2h}{R} \times 100\%$$
  • (iv) Inverse Square Variation Outside Earth:
    If $(R + h) = r$, then from (16), $g' = \frac{GM}{r^2}$ or $g' \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$. Thus the variation of $g'$ with $r$ is a rectangular hyperbola curve (Fig. 8.10b).
Textbook Sample Problem

How much above the surface of earth does the acceleration due to gravity reduce by $64\%$ of its value on the surface of earth? Radius of earth $= 6400\text{ km}$.

Solution: Since acceleration due to gravity reduces by $64\%$, the remaining value of $g'$ there is $100\% - 64\% = 36\%$ of $g$: $$g' = \frac{36}{100}g$$
Since the change is large, we MUST use Exact Equation (17): $$g' = g \frac{R^2}{(R+h)^2} \implies \frac{36}{100}g = g\frac{R^2}{(R+h)^2}$$
Taking square root on both sides: $$\frac{6}{10} = \frac{R}{R+h} \implies 6R + 6h = 10R \implies 6h = 4R$$ $$h = \frac{2}{3}R = \frac{2 \times 6.4 \times 10^6}{3} = \mathbf{4.27 \times 10^6\text{ m}} \text{ (or } 4267\text{ km)}$$
Section 8.20 (b) • Effect of Depth

Variation of Acceleration Due to Gravity ($g$) with Depth ($d$)

What happens when we go inside a deep mine or tunnel below the surface of the Earth to a depth $d$? Even though we get closer to the Earth's centre, the effective mass of the Earth attracting us decreases from $M$ to $M'$ (only the inner sphere of radius $R - d$ exerts a net downward pull, while the outer spherical shell of thickness $d$ exerts zero net force!). As a result, $g$ decreases linearly with depth and becomes zero at the centre of the Earth.

Section 8.20 (b) • Complete Textbook Derivation

Step-by-Step Derivation: Effect of Depth ($d$)

Step 1: Express Surface Gravity $g$ in Terms of Density $\rho$

Consider the Earth to be a homogeneous sphere of radius $R$ and mass $M$ with centre at $O$. Let $g$ be the value of acceleration due to gravity at a point $A$ on the surface of the Earth (Fig. 8.11a). Then $g = \frac{GM}{R^2}$.
If $\rho$ is the uniform density of material of the Earth, then $M = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho$.

$$g = \frac{G \times \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho}{R^2} = \frac{4}{3}\pi G R \rho \qquad \text{...(19)}$$
Step 2: Divide Earth into Inner Sphere & Outer Shell at Depth $d$

Let $g'$ be the acceleration due to gravity at point $B$ at a depth $d$ below the surface of the Earth. The distance of point $B$ from the centre $O$ is $(R - d)$. The Earth can be supposed to be made of:

  • (i) A smaller inner sphere of radius $(R - d)$, and
  • (ii) A spherical shell of thickness $d$.

The body at $B$ is inside the spherical shell of thickness $d$, so the force on the body due to this outer spherical shell is zero. The body at $B$ is on the surface of the smaller sphere of radius $(R - d)$, so the force is just as if the entire mass $M'$ of the smaller sphere is concentrated at the centre $O$.

Step 3: Express $g'$ at Depth $d$ in Terms of Density $\rho$

Since $g' = \frac{G M'}{(R - d)^2}$ and the mass of the inner sphere is $M' = \frac{4}{3}\pi (R - d)^3 \rho$:

$$g' = \frac{G \times \frac{4}{3}\pi (R - d)^3 \rho}{(R - d)^2} = \frac{4}{3}\pi G (R - d)\rho \qquad \text{...(20)}$$
Step 4: Divide Equation (20) by Equation (19)

Dividing (20) by (19), we get:

$$\frac{g'}{g} = \frac{\frac{4}{3}\pi G (R - d)\rho}{\frac{4}{3}\pi G R \rho} = \frac{R - d}{R} = 1 - \frac{d}{R}$$
Exact Depth Formula (Equation 21)
$$g' = g\left(1 - \frac{d}{R}\right) \qquad \text{...(21)}$$

Note: Unlike the altitude approximation, $g' = g(1 - d/R)$ is an exact equation for all depths $d \le R$ (assuming uniform density).

Special Case: At the Centre of the Earth

At the centre of the Earth, depth $d = R$, and let $g' = g_0$. Substituting $d = R$ in Equation (21):

$$g_0 = g\left(1 - \frac{R}{R}\right) = 0 \qquad \text{...(22)}$$

Therefore, the weight of a body of mass $m$ at the centre of the Earth is $m g_0 = 0$, but the mass $m$ of the body will NOT be zero. Thus, the value of $g$ is maximum at the Earth's surface and becomes zero at the centre of the Earth.

Important Notes & Unified $r$-Relation
• Decrease in value of $g$ with depth $d$: $g - g' = \frac{d g}{R}$
• Fractional decrease (Eq. 23): $\frac{g - g'}{g} = \frac{d}{R}$
• Percentage decrease (Eq. 24): $\frac{g - g'}{g} \times 100 = \frac{d}{R} \times 100\%$
Variation of $g$ with Distance $r$ from Centre $O$:
1. Inside the Earth ($r < R$, where $r = R - d$): $$g_d = g\left(\frac{R - d}{R}\right) = \frac{g r}{R} \implies \mathbf{g_d \propto r}$$ (Straight line passing through origin $O$)
2. Outside the Earth ($r > R$, where $r = R + h$): $$g_h = \frac{g R^2}{(R + h)^2} = \frac{g R^2}{r^2} \implies \mathbf{g_h \propto \frac{1}{r^2}}$$ (Inverse-square curve decreasing towards $\infty$)
Section 8.20 (c) • Effect of Shape of Earth

Variation of Acceleration Due to Gravity ($g$) with Shape of Earth

Earth is not a perfect sphere! It is an oblate spheroid—flattened at the poles and bulging out at the equator. Because the equatorial radius $R_e$ is about $21\text{ km}$ greater than the polar radius $R_p$, and $g \propto \frac{1}{R^2}$, the value of acceleration due to gravity is least at the equator and maximum at the poles.

Section 8.20 (c) • Textbook Explanation & Equations

Why Does $g$ Increase from Equator to Pole?

1. Oblate Spheroid Geometry of Earth (Fig. 8.13)

Earth is not a perfect sphere. It is flattened at the poles and bulges out at the equator. The equatorial radius $R_e$ of the Earth is about $21\text{ km}$ greater than the polar radius $R_p$:

$$R_e > R_p \quad \text{and} \quad R_e - R_p \approx 21\text{ km}$$
2. Inverse-Square Dependence on Radius

We know that acceleration due to gravity on the surface is given by: $$g = \frac{G M}{R^2}$$ Since universal gravitational constant $G$ and mass of the Earth $M$ are constants:

$$g \propto \frac{1}{R^2}$$
3. Comparing Gravity at Poles ($g_p$) and Equator ($g_e$)

At the equator, acceleration due to gravity is $g_e = \frac{GM}{R_e^2}$, and at the poles, $g_p = \frac{GM}{R_p^2}$.
Since $R_e > R_p$:

Key Conclusion
$$g_p > g_e \quad \left(\frac{g_p}{g_e} = \frac{R_e^2}{R_p^2}\right)$$

Thus, we conclude that the value of $g$ is least at the equator and maximum at the pole. It means the value of acceleration due to gravity increases as we go from the equator to the pole.

Quantitative Fact (From Reference Book)
Difference at Sea Level (Due to Shape)
$g_p - g_e = 1.80\text{ cm s}^{-2}$
($= 0.018\text{ m s}^{-2}$)

At sea level, the value of $g$ at the pole is greater than its value at the equator by $1.80\text{ cm s}^{-2}$ due to the non-spherical shape of the Earth alone.

classic Conceptual Application: Sugar Bought at Equator vs. Poles

If a person buys $1\text{ kg}$ of sugar at the equator and weighs it using a spring balance at the poles, it will weigh slightly more at the poles because $W = mg$ and $g_p > g_e$. (However, a beam balance compares masses and will read the same at both places!)
